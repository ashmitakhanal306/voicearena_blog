The Naturalness Gap: What 44,000 Human Votes Reveal About TTS Beyond English

We ran the largest multilingual pairwise TTS evaluation to date — 44,387 human votes across six languages, nine models, and four content domains. The finding is not a surprise, but the size of the problem is.

Why Multilingual Evaluation Changes the Picture

Most published TTS benchmarks evaluate English. A few cover one or two additional languages. Almost none run the same models simultaneously across languages with enough scale to make statistically meaningful comparisons — and almost none give you the human comments that explain why one model was preferred.

This creates a blind spot. A model can perform well on English naturalness benchmarks, achieve a strong Elo rating in English arenas, and still sound noticeably robotic to a native Hindi or Vietnamese speaker. Without a multilingual evaluation running the same models against the same criteria, that gap stays invisible.

VoiceArena is built to close that gap. This report covers the current evaluation round: 44,387 pairwise human votes across six languages — English (en-US), Hindi (hi-IN), Arabic (ar-MSA), Japanese (ja-JP), Portuguese Brazilian (pt-BR), and Vietnamese (vi-VN) — using a controlled set of 9 frontier TTS models. Approximately 49% of votes include written evaluator comments explaining the decision. The result is a dataset that lets you compare models not just on who won, but on how and why they lost.

How VoiceArena Measures Preference

VoiceArena uses pairwise evaluation. A human evaluator receives the same sentence, given to two different models. Both models generate audio. The evaluator listens to both and selects which they prefer — or marks a tie if neither is clearly better. Each vote produces one of three outcomes: a_wins, b_wins, or tie.

In addition to voting, evaluators tag specific defects on either or both outputs. The defect taxonomy covers: unnatural_robotic_voice, mild_mispronunciation, severe_mispronunciation, irregular_pacing, hallucination_extra_content, missing_word, and noise_distortion. Evaluators also write free-text comments in their native language, providing the qualitative signal that raw win rates cannot capture.

The evaluation sentences span four content domains — customer support and contact center, general conversation and AI assistants, media and entertainment, and content creation and education — with sub-buckets designed to include the kinds of inputs that routinely break TTS: numbers, alphanumeric identifiers, mixed-language text, filler words, and emotionally inflected speech. All model pairings are anonymized to the evaluator.

A note on defect rates: Defect tags are applied independently by each evaluator and represent perceptual observations, not automated metrics. They are not mutually exclusive — a single output can receive multiple tags. Defect rates in this report are normalized by model appearances (total number of votes in which the model participated on that side), not by total votes.

The Naturalness Gap: Robotic Voice Across Languages

The single most pervasive failure mode in this dataset is unnatural or robotic voice — the judgment that a model's output sounds like a machine rather than a person. In English, most frontier models have brought this defect rate to manageable levels. Outside English, the numbers look very different.

Two patterns stand out immediately.

First, the language sensitivity of naturalness varies enormously across models. Gemini's robotic voice rate stays between 5.9% and 9.8% across all six languages — a difference of roughly 4 percentage points between its best and worst language. OpenAI's range is 18% to 49.2% — a 31-point spread. Microsoft Azure's range is 13.2% to 42.6% — a 29-point spread. For these models, "naturalness" is not a general capability that transfers across languages; it is an English-specific property that degrades sharply elsewhere.

Second, the worst-affected language varies by model. OpenAI collapses most severely in Vietnamese and Hindi. Azure collapses most severely in Japanese and Portuguese. These differences likely reflect differences in training data composition, prosody modeling for each language family, and how well each model has internalized the phonological constraints specific to each language.

The practical implication: A model's English naturalness score is not a reliable predictor of its behavior in other languages. Teams deploying TTS at scale in multilingual products are operating partially blind without language-specific evaluations using native speaker annotators — which is exactly what this data provides.

Cartesia's Pacing Problem in English

One anomaly worth flagging: Cartesia Sonic-3 shows an unusually high irregular pacing defect rate specifically in English — 32.5%, the highest of any model in any single language for this defect type. Its robotic voice rate in English is also 21.1%, higher than in any other language it was evaluated on.

Human comments in the English dataset repeatedly flag specific pacing failures: pauses inserted at wrong positions within tokens, rushed delivery of numbers, and rhythm breakdowns at special characters like underscores and hyphens. These patterns suggest a structural issue with how Cartesia handles the specific kinds of mixed-format content (usernames, identifiers, filler words) that appear frequently in the evaluation set — not a general voice quality problem, but a specific prosodic failure mode at symbol and number boundaries.

The Mispronunciation Wall in Arabic and Japanese

If naturalness is the leading failure mode in Hindi and Vietnamese, mispronunciation is the defining failure mode in Arabic and Japanese. These two language families impose phonological demands — Arabic's short vowel system and geminate consonants; Japanese's pitch accent, mora timing, and mixed script reading — that expose clear boundaries in current TTS capability.

Microsoft Azure's Japanese mispronunciation rate — 84.6% — deserves attention. In practical terms, this means that in nearly nine out of ten Japanese evaluations, annotators observed at least one pronunciation error in Azure's output. — Evaluator annotation, ja-JP STEM education evaluation

Arabic presents a different but equally significant challenge. The evaluation includes Modern Standard Arabic with diacritics marking vowel sounds that must be preserved in correct pronunciation. Models that perform well on undiacritized Arabic text still frequently fail on diacritized input, suggesting that diacritic-aware grapheme-to-phoneme conversion is not uniformly reliable across the models evaluated.

Notably, Gemini's mispronunciation rate in Arabic (14.8%) and Japanese (15.9%) is 3–5× lower than the next-best model in those languages. Given that Arabic and Japanese impose some of the highest demands on pronunciation systems, this gap suggests a meaningful difference in how well the underlying model has been trained on non-Latin script phonology — not just surface-level text-to-phoneme lookup, but prosodic and contextual correctness.

Hallucination: When TTS Invents Content

Hallucination in large language models — generating content not grounded in the input — is well-studied. Hallucination in TTS is not. The VoiceArena defect taxonomy includes hallucination_extra_content, defined as audio output that contains words, sounds, or content not present in the source text. Human evaluators flagged this defect 5,236 times across the full dataset.

The language distribution of hallucination is highly uneven.

xAI Grok TTS hallucinates extra content in 18% of Arabic evaluations, compared to 1.4% in English. In absolute terms: in roughly one in five Arabic evaluations where Grok participated, a human listener flagged something in the audio that was not in the source text. The hallucination types documented in comments include: invented numbers, substituted words, added filler sounds, and cases where the model vocalized punctuation as content.

xAI Grok TTS ar-MSA hallucination_extra_content
"هناك صوت ضحكات لم تكن في النص الاصلي" (There are laugh sounds that were not in the original text.)
— Evaluator annotation, ar-MSA media and entertainment evaluation
xAI Grok TTS ar-MSA hallucination_extra_content + severe_mispronunciation
"Wrong number in saying 1422. She said it as '2na410'. Extra content in adding the sound 'أه' [a filler vocalization not in the source text]."
— Evaluator annotation, ar-MSA customer support evaluation

These are not edge cases. The 18% rate means that in the Arabic portion of this evaluation, Grok TTS was flagged for hallucination more often than it was flagged for any defect type other than mispronunciation. This has direct implications for deployment in Arabic customer service, where numbers, dates, reference codes, and transaction identifiers must be reproduced exactly.

Why is hallucination so much higher in Arabic? There are several plausible explanations: sparser Arabic training data leading to less reliable text-to-phoneme alignment, greater ambiguity in unvoweled Arabic script (where a sequence of consonants can resolve to multiple words), and higher sensitivity to diacritic presence or absence causing the model to substitute or interpolate content. Disambiguating these causes requires deeper internal analysis than this evaluation can provide — but the behavioral signal is clear.

The Hardest Content: Customer Support and Structured Data

Not all content is equally hard for TTS. The evaluation was designed with this in mind: sentences span four broad categories and more than twenty sub-buckets, from short conversational exchanges to IVR scripts with alphanumeric identifiers.

The data is consistent: customer support and contact center content generates the highest defect rates in the evaluation, across all languages.

Drilling into sub-buckets, the highest defect rates in the entire evaluation are: Banking duplicate-charge dispute (2.33 defects/vote), Commercial license PA (2.35 defects/vote), AI assistant — financial transfer confirmation (2.15 defects/vote), and E-commerce — return window expired (2.06 defects/vote). All are contexts where natural delivery of numbers, account identifiers, and dates is critical to the information being understood.

Human comments from these categories are highly specific. The most common failure pattern is number reading: models collapsing digit sequences into compound numbers ("forty-four" instead of "four, four"), merging alphanumeric identifiers ("CHI" spoken as "ki" instead of "C-H-I"), or switching between reading registers mid-string (saying "USF" as a word rather than spelling it).

"The model merged the letters CHI in the booking ID together. Instead of saying them individually, as C, H, I, it pronounced them as 'ki'. It also said the numbers together as 'forty three fifty one' when they should have been said individually as four, three, five, one."
— Evaluator annotation, en-US customer support evaluation
Cartesia Sonic-3 en-US irregular_pacing
"The audio is clear. The model pauses in between saying 'maddie' and 'underscore' which affects the rhythm and throws off the pacing and makes it sound robotic at that part of the clip."
— Evaluator annotation, en-US customer support evaluation

These failures are not cosmetic. In an IVR or customer support voice assistant, misread account numbers directly break functionality. The fact that these failures appear consistently — across models, across languages, across annotators — suggests a persistent gap in how current TTS systems handle structured data embedded in natural language.

What the Human Comments Reveal

The VoiceArena vote log contains 21,755 annotated votes — evaluations where the human rater wrote a comment in addition to selecting a winner and tagging defects. These comments are written in the language of evaluation, by native speakers, and they reveal patterns that defect tags alone cannot capture.

Filler words and conversational register

Many evaluation sentences contain intentional filler words: uhh, umm, uh, and their equivalents in Arabic, Hindi, Vietnamese, and Japanese. These are not errors in the source text — they are deliberate markers of natural spoken register, testing whether models can render disfluency-as-naturalness rather than treating filler as something to remove or roboticize.

Comments on filler word handling appear repeatedly in the English dataset. Evaluators consistently distinguished between models that rendered fillers naturally (contributing to the impression of a real person) and models that either over-stretched them, delivered them at unnatural pitch, or inserted a pacing break that made the filler feel like a glitch rather than a breath.

OpenAI gpt-4o-mini-tts en-US irregular_pacing
"The audio stretches out the 'Uhhhhh' at the beginning a little too long, which is throwing off the pacing."
— Evaluator annotation, en-US customer support evaluation

In Vietnamese, annotators flagged the same class of failure in their own language:

OpenAI gpt-4o-mini-tts vi-VN unnatural_robotic_voice
"Ngữ điệu không giống người bản xứ" (Intonation doesn't sound like a native speaker.)
— Evaluator annotation, vi-VN general conversation evaluation

Tone and emotional register

Evaluators in the English dataset repeatedly referenced tone as distinct from pronunciation and pacing — the judgment that a voice sounds "over the top," "monotone," "too squeaky," or "like a TV commercial." These comments describe a real perceptual phenomenon: correct pronunciation and appropriate pacing are necessary but not sufficient for natural delivery. Register — how formal, warm, urgent, or casual the voice sounds — must also fit the context.

One evaluator, commenting on a customer support evaluation where a model won despite no technical flaws: "Well done, good pace, good pausing, the directions are clearly spoken and sound very natural." Another, losing the same matchup for a competing model: "It sounds like a voice you would hear in a TV commercial — too squeaky, too emotional for the subject." Both outputs were technically correct. The preference was about emotional calibration.

The Japanese evaluator pattern

Japanese evaluators wrote some of the most technically specific comments in the entire dataset. In a language where number reading conventions (reading four-digit numbers digit-by-digit versus as a compound), pitch accent, and loan-word pronunciation rules are complex and culturally important, evaluators flagged errors at a level of precision that reveals how high the bar is for a Japanese-native listener.

Microsoft Azure Dragon HD ja-JP severe_mispronunciation
"14時40分を、じゅうよんじよんじゅっぷん、と読んでいる。" (14:40 is being read as 'juu-yon-ji-yon-juppun', which is incorrect.)
— Evaluator annotation, ja-JP customer support evaluation

In 42.2% of Japanese evaluation matchups, both models in the comparison received at least one defect tag — the highest "both defective" rate of any language in the dataset. The practical meaning: in Japanese evaluation, it is not uncommon for a model to win a matchup while still being considered imperfect. The question becomes not "which model was good?" but "which model was less broken?"

Anatomy of a Consistent Winner

Gemini 3.1 Flash TTS wins more matchups than any other model in every language evaluated. The more interesting question is how it wins — and what that reveals about what separates a genuinely multilingual TTS system from one that has generalized English capabilities to some non-English settings.

The fourth number — 88.6% of Gemini's wins are clean, meaning the model won without any defect being tagged on its output — is particularly telling. It ranges from 82.1% in Japanese to 91.5% in English. For comparison, the models with the lowest defect-free win rates win significantly more of their matchups with at least one defect on the board. Gemini is not winning by being the less bad option; it is winning because its outputs are consistently cleaner.

Two properties of Gemini's profile stand out. First, its defect rates are stable across languages: robotic voice stays between 5.9% and 9.8%; mispronunciation stays between 4.7% and 15.9%. Other models show 3–5× variation across those same dimensions. Stability suggests that Gemini's multilingual training has produced representations of naturalness that generalize, rather than English-specific prosodic patterns that degrade on other phonological systems.

Second, Gemini leads most strongly in the languages where other models fail most badly: Arabic (61.5% win rate), Japanese (63.0% win rate). These are the languages with the highest mispronunciation rates, the most complex grapheme-to-phoneme rules, and the highest rates of "both models defective" matchups. Where the evaluation is hardest, the margin in Gemini's favor is largest.

This report does not explain why Gemini performs this way — that would require access to training details and architecture decisions not publicly available. What the data shows is that the performance gap is consistent, large, and not confined to a single dimension or language family.

What Needs to Happen Next

The findings from this evaluation converge on a few conclusions that are relevant for teams building, evaluating, or deploying TTS at scale.

English performance is not a proxy for multilingual performance. Across nearly every model in this evaluation, there is a large and meaningful degradation between English defect rates and defect rates in Hindi, Vietnamese, Arabic, and Japanese. Robotic voice — the most basic measure of naturalness — can more than double or triple when moving from English to Southeast Asian or South Asian languages. Teams that deploy models globally based on English evaluations are accepting an unknown performance risk in every non-English language they operate in.

The defect profile varies by language family in predictable ways. Arabic and Japanese expose mispronunciation and hallucination failures at rates that other languages do not. Hindi and Vietnamese expose naturalness failures. Portuguese and Arabic both expose robotic voice problems, but the leading models are different. This structure suggests that language-specific improvements are possible and that the failure modes are not randomly distributed — they reflect the phonological, orthographic, and prosodic demands of each language family.

Customer support and structured data remain an unsolved problem. The highest defect rates in the evaluation come from IVR, banking, and e-commerce content — content where numbers, identifiers, and transactional language must be rendered exactly. This is not a language-specific problem; it appears across all six languages. It represents a gap between what TTS models are good at (natural conversational speech) and what enterprise deployments actually require.

Human-annotated defect data is qualitatively different from automated metrics. Comments in this dataset document failure patterns that word error rate or character error rate would not capture: tone register mismatches, filler word timing failures, hallucinated laugh sounds, number reading register errors. These are the kinds of failures that make a product feel broken to real users. They do not reliably appear in automated TTS benchmarks.
